Emberdeep begins above ground, in a city built around a changing mine. You manage expeditions rather than a single hero. One crew explores while the others recover, train, forge equipment, and prepare to answer whatever the returning scouts uncover. If this is your first session, complete the short setup in [Getting Started](/guides/getting-started/) before using this guide as your map of the game.

<h2 id="the-seven-stations">The seven stations</h2>

The city has seven stations. Each converts time and materials into a different kind of readiness. You can inspect every station at any moment, but active jobs continue only when their requirements are met.

- **Gatehouse:** forms crews, assigns routes, and records retreat conditions.
- **Infirmary:** treats wounds and converts rest into recovered Resolve.
- **Drill Hall:** teaches techniques and tests formations without expedition risk.
- **Forge:** upgrades, repairs, and recycles gear while accumulating Heat.
- **Archive:** deciphers maps, relic scripts, and clues brought back from below.
- **Signal Tower:** reveals time-sensitive opportunities and warns of layer changes.
- **Storehouse:** tracks supplies, reserves, and the weight available to each crew.

Think of the stations as a connected workshop. Sending a wounded scout to training delays both recovery and the technique you hoped to learn. Filling the Forge with cosmetic improvements may leave no slot for an emergency repair. Before launching a crew, scan the seven station queues and ask which jobs will finish before that crew returns.

<h2 id="the-expedition-loop">The expedition loop in one sentence</h2>

Choose a purpose, assemble a crew, descend until the reward no longer justifies the risk, return, then turn what you learned into a safer next descent.

That sentence is the entire game, but each clause matters. A purpose might be mapping a branch, retrieving forge ore, testing a new formation, or reaching a layer gate. Without a purpose, it is easy to chase every glowing chamber and return with mismatched materials. Assembly means more than selecting your strongest portraits: it includes supplies, position, role coverage, and a retreat threshold. The descent is a sequence of imperfect choices made with partial information. Returning is an active decision, not what happens after success. Finally, learning means using maps and outcomes to change the next plan rather than repeating the same route with larger numbers.

Run three crews on staggered rhythms. The active crew earns information. The recovering crew turns wounds into readiness. The reserve crew handles a short opportunity or prepares for the newly revealed hazard. For detailed role patterns, see [Crew Building](/guides/squad-building/).

<h2 id="what-the-mine-does">What the mine does while you look away</h2>

The mine is a living schedule. Chambers can cool, flood, collapse, or become occupied between expeditions. A revealed route remains useful, but its exact cost may change. Signal Tower notices tell you which changes are predictable: a tremor may close a shortcut after two returns, while a spore bloom may make one branch dangerous now and valuable later. You are not expected to prevent every change. You are expected to decide which information is worth acting on.

Map certainty decays by layer, not all at once. The first rooms usually remain dependable; deeper rooms accumulate unknown modifiers. A scout or archive project can refresh certainty before a high-value expedition. This is why a partial mapping run can be productive even when it brings home little ore. It reduces the supplies and Resolve the next crew must spend on surprises.

Treat time-sensitive events as choices, not commands. Let one pass when the available crew lacks a keeper or when the Gatehouse would be left empty. The rotation creates future opportunities.

<h2 id="resolve-is-the-clock">Resolve is the real clock</h2>

Health tells you whether a recruit can survive the next hit. Resolve tells you whether the crew can continue making reliable choices. It falls through darkness, failed checks, conflicting traits, and certain injuries. Low-Resolve crews spend more supplies, lose formation discipline, and turn minor hazards into cascading failures. That makes Resolve your practical expedition timer even when every health bar looks comfortable.

Set a retreat threshold at the Gatehouse. For an unfamiliar route, six shared Resolve is a sensible early warning and four is a firm return signal. Experienced crews can bend those numbers when a safe exit is one chamber away, but they should do so for a named reward, not from momentum. Bring one tool that restores or protects Resolve, and know which member carries it. Supplies buried in the wrong pack do not help during a panic event.

On return, do not heal only visible wounds. A physically healthy crew with exhausted Resolve belongs in the Infirmary, Drill Hall observation seats, or a low-pressure Archive job. Rotate in the reserve instead of forcing a favorite team through a second descent.

<h2 id="power-beyond-recruits">Power that is not a new recruit</h2>

Recruit rarity is obvious, so new captains often overvalue it. Most lasting power comes from systems that improve several people or reduce a repeated cost. Invest in this order unless a route presents a specific counterexample:

1. **Reliable information.** Upgrade lantern reach, Archive speed, and map certainty so decisions begin with fewer unknowns.
2. **Shared infrastructure.** Improve treatment capacity, storage, and station throughput; every crew benefits from shorter queues.
3. **Flexible gear.** Forge items that can move between roles and answer common hazards before making narrow signature pieces.
4. **Focused mastery.** Train techniques and refine specialist relics after the rotation can support the time and material cost.

This order does not forbid exciting equipment. It is a safeguard against owning one exceptional crew and two liabilities. When evaluating any purchase, count how many planned expeditions will use it.

<h2 id="a-sane-session">A sane session</h2>

Begin with a two-minute city check. Collect completed jobs, read only urgent Signal notices, and inspect the condition of all three crews. Choose one session goal, such as opening a layer gate or stabilizing the gear rotation. Then assign station work that supports that goal before entering the mine. This prevents the returning crew from discovering that the Infirmary and Forge are already blocked by low-priority jobs.

During an expedition, pause at branch points. Compare the original purpose, current Resolve, exit distance, and new information. Continue only if at least three of those still support the descent. Mark valuable routes you decline; a reserve crew may be able to take them while the first team recovers.

End after a return rather than midway through preparation. Repair essential gear, place wounded recruits, queue one research or forge job, and leave the next crew assembled at the Gatehouse. A clean stopping point makes the next session begin with a decision instead of housekeeping.

<h2 id="what-not-to-do">What not to do</h2>

Do not merge every strong recruit into one permanent team. That team will monopolize gear and treatment, and one injury will halt your progress. Do not spend rare materials merely to clear an upgrade marker; wait until an upgrade solves a route problem. Do not treat a revealed chamber as a promise that it will remain unchanged. Check the Signal Tower and map certainty before committing expensive supplies.

Most importantly, do not confuse reaching deeper rooms with progressing. A run that consumes the reserve crew's gear, fills the Infirmary, and leaves no ore for repairs can move the map forward while moving the campaign backward. Sustainable progress leaves at least one healthy crew, one useful station job, and one affordable next objective. The mine rewards boldness, but the city rewards continuity, and you need both to reach the vaults beneath Emberdeep.
